Solar System Studies · No. 2

The Terrestrial Planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars

A review article

November 2026

Abstract

The four planets closest to the Sun are small, dense bodies of rock and metal. They formed inside the frost line, where only refractory material could condense, and as a result none of them grew large enough to hold a substantial envelope of hydrogen and helium. This review compares their orbits, surfaces and atmospheres, and considers why worlds built from similar ingredients evolved into an airless, cratered Mercury, a hot and pressurised Venus, a temperate and inhabited Earth, and a cold, thin-aired Mars.

Keywords: terrestrial planets, frost line, planetary atmospheres, habitable zone, comparative planetology

1. Introduction

The inner Solar System contains four terrestrial planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. They share a broadly similar structure, with a metallic core, a rocky mantle and a solid crust, yet their present conditions differ enormously. Comparing them is one of the most productive approaches in planetary science, because differences in size, distance from the Sun and atmospheric history can be tested against a common starting point.

2. The Frost Line and the Origin of Rocky Worlds

In the disc of gas and dust that surrounded the young Sun, temperature fell with distance. The frost line marks the boundary beyond which volatile compounds such as water, ammonia and methane could remain solid as ices. It lay at roughly 5 AU, about five times the distance of the Earth from the Sun. Inside it, only metals and silicates condensed, and these refractory materials made up only a small fraction of the nebula. The planets that formed there therefore stayed relatively small and could not capture large amounts of nebular gas, in contrast with the giant planets that grew beyond the line.

3. Mercury

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun, following a markedly eccentric orbit that ranges from about 0.31 to 0.59 AU. It completes one orbit in just under 88 days. With almost no atmosphere to retain or distribute heat, its surface temperature swings from about minus 170 degrees Celsius at night to around 420 degrees Celsius in daylight. Its heavily cratered surface preserves a long record of impacts. Mercury has no moons and no rings.

4. Venus

Venus is similar to the Earth in size, which once led to it being described as a sister planet. Its conditions, however, are extreme. A dense atmosphere composed mainly of carbon dioxide produces a surface pressure about 90 times that at sea level on Earth, and a powerful greenhouse effect keeps the surface above 400 degrees Celsius, hotter than Mercury despite being nearly twice as far from the Sun. Venus rotates in the opposite direction to most planets, a retrograde rotation whose origin is still debated. Like Mercury, it has no moons.

5. Earth

The Earth orbits at an average distance of 1 AU and is the largest of the terrestrial planets. Its atmosphere is about 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen, and liquid water covers most of its surface. It is the only body known to support life. The Earth has a single natural satellite, the Moon, whose diameter is about a quarter of the Earth's, an unusually large ratio for a planet and its moon.

5.1 The habitable zone

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star at which a planet with a suitable atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface. In the Solar System, the Earth and Mars are the only planets that orbit within it. Their differing fates show that position alone does not guarantee habitability: mass, atmospheric retention and geological activity matter as well.

6. Mars

Mars orbits between about 1.38 and 1.67 AU and takes almost 687 days to circle the Sun. Its radius is roughly half that of the Earth. Its atmosphere is very thin, with a surface pressure about 0.6 percent of the Earth's, so liquid water cannot persist on the surface today, although landforms indicate that it once flowed. Mars has two small, irregular moons, Phobos, with a mean radius of about 11 kilometres, and Deimos, with a mean radius of about 6 kilometres.

7. Discussion

The contrasts among the terrestrial planets can be traced to a few factors. Mercury is small and close to the Sun, so it lost any early atmosphere. Venus retained a thick atmosphere that trapped heat in a runaway greenhouse. Mars, smaller than the Earth and with weaker gravity, lost much of its atmosphere and cooled. The Earth occupies a middle ground, large enough to retain an atmosphere and geologically active enough to recycle carbon over long periods, which has helped to stabilise its climate. None of the four planets has a ring system.

8. Conclusion

The terrestrial planets formed from the same scarce, rocky material inside the frost line, yet diverged into very different worlds. Their study helps to explain the conditions that make a planet habitable, and provides a framework for interpreting the many rocky planets now being discovered around other stars.
