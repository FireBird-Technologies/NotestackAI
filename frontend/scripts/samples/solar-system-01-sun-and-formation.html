The Sun and the Formation of the Solar System: From Nebular Collapse to Stellar Old Age

A review article

November 2026

Abstract

The Solar System formed about 4.568 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of a fragment of a molecular cloud. Nearly all of that material settled into the Sun, which today holds 99.86 percent of the system's mass, while the remainder flattened into a disc from which the planets grew. This review summarises the evidence for that origin, the physical character of the Sun as a main-sequence star, the dynamical events that rearranged the young planetary system, and the expected evolution of the Sun over the next several billion years.

Keywords: nebular hypothesis, protoplanetary disc, main sequence, stellar evolution, planetary migration

1. Introduction

The Solar System is the gravitationally bound system of the Sun and the bodies that orbit it: eight planets, their natural satellites, dwarf planets, and a very large population of smaller objects. Understanding how it formed is central to planetary science, because the conditions of that first period fixed the composition, spacing and orbits that are observed today. This paper reviews the formation of the system and the life of its central star.

2. Dating the Solar System

The age of the Solar System is measured from the oldest solid materials known, calcium and aluminium rich inclusions found in primitive meteorites. Radiometric dating of these inclusions gives an age of 4,568.2 million years, with an uncertainty of a few hundred thousand years. Because these grains condensed directly from the hot gas surrounding the young Sun, their age is taken as the start of the system's history.

3. Collapse of the Presolar Nebula

The prevailing explanation, the nebular hypothesis, holds that a region of a giant molecular cloud, probably several light-years across, collapsed under its own gravity. As it contracted, conservation of angular momentum made it spin faster, and the collapsing material flattened into a rotating protoplanetary disc roughly 200 astronomical units (AU) in diameter, with a hot, dense protostar at its centre.

Most of the mass fell inward to form the Sun. The rest remained in the disc, where dust grains collided and stuck together, building up planetesimals and eventually planets. Temperature controlled what could condense where. Close to the young Sun only rock and metal could stay solid, so the inner planets formed from a scarce supply of refractory material and remained small. In the first million years, heat from accretion also drove volatile hydrocarbons out of the inner disc, a boundary sometimes called the soot line, which has since moved inside the orbit of the Earth.

4. The Sun as a Star

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star of spectral class G2V. It has a radius of about 700,000 kilometres, or 0.0047 AU, and is composed of roughly 98 percent hydrogen and helium by mass, the same proportions found in Jupiter and Saturn. Its mass, about 332,900 times that of the Earth, dominates the dynamics of every other body in the system.

Hydrogen fusion in the solar core began within about 50 million years of the initial collapse. The young Sun was considerably fainter than it is today, shining at about 70 percent of its present brightness. Its luminosity has increased slowly ever since, a trend that continues as helium accumulates in the core.

4.1 Energy and the solar wind

Energy released by fusion travels outward and leaves the Sun as light and as the solar wind, a continuous stream of charged particles. The solar wind inflates a vast bubble, the heliosphere, which surrounds the entire planetary system and marks the region in which the Sun's influence exceeds that of interstellar space.

5. Rearrangement of the Young System

The planets did not necessarily form where they orbit today. Simulations known collectively as the Nice model propose that gravitational encounters between the giant planets and a disc of remaining planetesimals caused the giants to migrate, triggering a period of orbital instability that left them in their present positions. A related idea, the grand tack hypothesis, suggests that Jupiter first migrated inward and then reversed direction, scattering much of the material in what is now the asteroid belt. Such migrations may explain the Late Heavy Bombardment, an epoch of intense impacts recorded on the Moon and the inner planets.

Over very long timescales the system is chaotic: small perturbations grow, and the precise positions of the planets cannot be predicted indefinitely. External influences also play a role. A star passes within about 0.8 light-years of the Sun roughly once every 100,000 years, disturbing the most distant objects that orbit it.

6. The Future of the Sun

The Sun is roughly halfway through a main-sequence lifetime of about ten billion years. In approximately five billion years the hydrogen in its core will be exhausted. The core will contract while the outer layers expand, and the Sun will become a red giant up to about 260 times its present diameter, with a cooler surface of around 2,600 kelvin. Mercury and Venus are expected to be engulfed and vaporised, and the Earth and Mars will become uninhabitable.

After a relatively brief phase of helium burning, the Sun will shed its outer layers, possibly forming a planetary nebula. What remains will be a white dwarf: an exposed stellar core with about half of the Sun's original mass compressed into a body roughly the size of the Earth, which will cool and fade over an extremely long time.

7. Conclusion

The Solar System began as a collapsing cloud of gas and dust, and the distribution of temperature in the resulting disc shaped the rocky inner planets and the massive outer ones. The Sun, which captured almost all of the original material, has brightened steadily since fusion began and will eventually swell, shed its envelope and end as a white dwarf. The dynamical upheavals of the early system, recorded in impact craters and in the orbits of small bodies, remain an active field of research.
