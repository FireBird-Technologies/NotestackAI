Small Bodies of the Solar System: Asteroids, Dwarf Planets, Comets and Dust

A review article

November 2026

Abstract

Alongside the eight planets, the Solar System contains more than a million known minor planets, thousands of comets and an uncounted quantity of meteoroids and dust. These small bodies are concentrated in a few broad reservoirs: the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, the Trojan populations that share planetary orbits, and the Kuiper belt and scattered disc beyond Neptune. This review describes each population, the dwarf planets found among them, and the comets and dust that move between them. Because many small bodies have changed little since they formed, they preserve evidence of the early Solar System.

Keywords: asteroid belt, Kuiper belt, dwarf planets, comets, meteoroids, Trojans

1. Introduction

Small bodies are the leftovers of planet formation: planetesimals that never merged into planets, or fragments of larger objects broken apart by collisions. Their orbits and compositions record the conditions and events of the early Solar System, including the migration of the giant planets. They are also numerous. Catalogues list more than 1.5 million minor planets and several thousand comets, and new discoveries are made continually.

2. The Asteroid Belt

The main asteroid belt occupies the region between about 2.3 and 3.3 AU from the Sun, between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter. It may contain millions of objects larger than one kilometre across, yet its total mass is unlikely to exceed one thousandth of the Earth's. The gravity of Jupiter is thought to have prevented the material here from assembling into a planet.

The largest object in the belt is Ceres, about 940 kilometres across and the only dwarf planet in the region. The next largest are Vesta and Pallas. Together these few bodies account for a large fraction of the belt's total mass.

3. Trojans

Trojans are small bodies that share the orbit of a larger body, gathered around two stable points 60 degrees ahead of it and 60 degrees behind it, known as the L4 and L5 Lagrange points. Every planet except Mercury is known to have at least one Trojan companion. Jupiter's Trojans are especially numerous, with a population that may rival that of the main asteroid belt.

4. The Kuiper Belt and the Scattered Disc

Beyond Neptune, between about 30 and 50 AU, lies the Kuiper belt, a broad ring of icy bodies. It is estimated to contain more than 100,000 objects larger than 50 kilometres across, although its total mass is small, perhaps between one tenth and one hundredth of the Earth's. The population drops sharply beyond about 50 AU, a feature known as the Kuiper cliff.

Overlapping the Kuiper belt and extending far beyond it is the scattered disc, whose members follow eccentric orbits that can reach several hundred AU. These orbits are thought to result from past gravitational encounters with Neptune, and the scattered disc is regarded as the main source of short-period comets.

5. Dwarf Planets

A dwarf planet is a body massive enough to have pulled itself into a nearly round shape, but which has not cleared its orbital neighbourhood of other material. Five objects are formally recognised as dwarf planets: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris. Several others, including Orcus, Quaoar, Gonggong and Sedna, are generally considered to qualify as well.

Pluto, which orbits between about 29.7 and 49.3 AU, was regarded as the ninth planet until 2006, when a formal definition of a planet led to its reclassification. It has five known moons. Eris, on a more distant and eccentric orbit, is about a quarter more massive than Pluto. Sedna follows one of the most extreme orbits known, taking roughly 11,400 years to circle the Sun.

6. Centaurs and Comets

Centaurs are icy bodies orbiting among the giant planets, with orbits between roughly 5.5 and 30 AU. They are thought to be objects in transition, scattered inward from the Kuiper belt. Comets are small bodies, typically a few kilometres across, made largely of volatile ices. When a comet approaches the Sun, these ices vaporise and form a glowing coma and tail. Short-period comets, with orbits of less than 200 years, are believed to come from the Kuiper belt and scattered disc; long-period comets, with orbits lasting thousands of years, from the much more distant Oort cloud.

7. Meteoroids and Dust

The smallest solid bodies are meteoroids, defined by the International Astronomical Union since 2017 as objects between about 30 micrometres and one metre across. Smaller particles are classed as dust. When a meteoroid enters the Earth's atmosphere it produces a streak of light called a meteor, and meteor showers occur when the Earth crosses streams of particles moving on parallel paths. A cloud of fine dust in the inner Solar System scatters sunlight to produce the faint zodiacal light, while a second dust cloud between about 10 and 40 AU is probably supplied by collisions in the Kuiper belt.

8. Conclusion

The small bodies of the Solar System are diverse in size, composition and orbit, but together they form a record of how the planets assembled and moved. Continued surveys and spacecraft visits, including those that have returned samples from asteroids and comets, are steadily refining that record.
