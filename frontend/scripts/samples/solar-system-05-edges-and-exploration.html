The Edges of the Solar System: The Heliosphere, the Oort Cloud and the History of Exploration

A review article

November 2026

Abstract

Where the Solar System ends depends on the definition used. The solar wind carves out a bubble, the heliosphere, that extends past the orbits of the planets; the Sun's gravity holds a distant cloud of comets far beyond that; and the system as a whole moves through the Milky Way as one member of a vast galaxy. This review describes these outer boundaries, the Solar System's place in its galactic surroundings, and the scientific and spaceflight milestones through which this picture was assembled.

Keywords: heliosphere, heliopause, Oort cloud, Hill sphere, Milky Way, space exploration

1. Introduction

For most of history the Solar System was understood as the Sun, the Moon and the visible planets. Today its boundaries are drawn in several ways: by the reach of the solar wind, by the extent of objects bound to the Sun by gravity, and by the transition to the interstellar medium. Each boundary lies at a very different distance, and each has been studied by different means, from theoretical models to direct measurements by spacecraft.

2. The Heliosphere

The Sun continuously emits a stream of charged particles, the solar wind, which inflates a bubble in the surrounding interstellar medium called the heliosphere. Moving outward, the solar wind first slows abruptly at the termination shock, at about 80 to 100 AU in the direction of the Sun's motion and perhaps around 200 AU in the opposite direction. Beyond the shock the wind becomes compressed and turbulent, forming a region called the heliosheath.

The outer boundary of the heliosphere is the heliopause, where the pressure of the solar wind is balanced by that of the interstellar medium. It is regarded as the beginning of interstellar space. Spacecraft have detected it at about 120 AU. The heliosphere is not symmetrical: its northern edge extends several AU farther than its southern edge, and a bow shock in the interstellar medium may lie at roughly 230 AU.

3. The Oort Cloud

Far beyond the heliopause lies the Oort cloud, a hypothesised spherical reservoir of icy bodies thought to extend from about 2,000 AU to as far as 200,000 AU, with most of its material between 3,000 and 100,000 AU. It has never been observed directly. Its existence is inferred from long-period comets, which arrive from all directions on orbits that take them thousands of years to complete. The Oort cloud is so distant that passing stars and the tide of the galaxy can disturb its members and send some of them inward. The Voyager 1 spacecraft, currently the most distant human-made object, is not expected to reach the inner Oort cloud for about 300 years.

4. The Gravitational Boundary

Another way to define the edge of the Solar System is by gravity. The Hill sphere of the Sun is the region within which its gravity dominates over that of the galaxy as a whole, extending to roughly 230,000 AU, or more than three light-years. The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, lies only slightly farther away, at about 4.25 light-years, and is known to host at least one planet.

5. The Solar System in the Milky Way

The Solar System is located in the Milky Way, a barred spiral galaxy about 100,000 light-years across containing more than 100 billion stars. The Sun lies in the Orion arm, also called the Local Spur, at a distance of about 26,000 light-years from the galactic centre. It moves at roughly 220 kilometres per second and completes one orbit of the galaxy in about 230 to 240 million years. The plane of the planets is inclined by about 60 degrees to the plane of the galaxy.

6. A History of Discovery

Modern understanding of the Solar System began with the heliocentric model of Nicolaus Copernicus, which placed the Sun at the centre. Johannes Kepler refined it by showing that planetary orbits are ellipses. In the seventeenth century Galileo Galilei and Simon Marius observed the four largest moons of Jupiter, and Christiaan Huygens discovered Titan and correctly described the shape of Saturn's rings. In 1705 Edmond Halley recognised that a bright comet returned every 75 to 76 years. Friedrich Bessel measured the parallax of a star in 1838, confirming the immense distances between stars, and in 1846 Neptune was located from its effect on the orbit of Uranus.

7. The Space Age

From the 1960s, telescopes in space and robotic probes transformed the field. By 1989 spacecraft had visited all eight planets. The Apollo missions landed astronauts on the Moon, and later missions returned samples from comets and asteroids and visited the dwarf planets Pluto and Ceres. Missions such as the Voyager probes used gravity assists from the giant planets to reach the outer Solar System, and the two Voyagers have since crossed the heliopause. In 2006 a formal definition of the term planet led to the reclassification of Pluto as a dwarf planet.

8. Conclusion

The Solar System has no single edge. The solar wind gives way to interstellar space at around 120 AU, the comets of the Oort cloud may extend more than a thousand times farther, and the Sun's gravity competes with that of its neighbours at distances of light-years. Centuries of observation and decades of spaceflight have made it possible to describe these boundaries, while many questions, particularly about the Oort cloud, remain open.
