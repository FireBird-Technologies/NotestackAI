Solar System Studies · No. 3

The Giant Planets: Gas Giants, Ice Giants and Their Systems of Rings and Moons

A review article

November 2026

Abstract

Beyond the frost line lie the four giant planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, which together contain about 99 percent of the mass that orbits the Sun. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants dominated by hydrogen and helium; Uranus and Neptune are ice giants richer in water, ammonia and methane. This review describes their composition and orbits, their ring systems and their extensive families of moons, and the evidence that their orbits changed early in the history of the Solar System.

Keywords: gas giants, ice giants, planetary rings, natural satellites, planetary migration

1. Introduction

The outer planets are far larger than the terrestrial worlds and differ from them in almost every respect. They lack solid surfaces of the kind found on Earth, have deep atmospheres, and are surrounded by rings and by moons numbering in the dozens or hundreds. Their great mass also makes them the dominant gravitational influence on the smaller bodies of the Solar System.

2. Formation Beyond the Frost Line

Outside the frost line, at roughly 5 AU from the Sun, volatile compounds could freeze into solid ice. Ices were far more abundant than rock and metal, so solid cores grew quickly to large masses. Once a core was massive enough, it could capture hydrogen and helium directly from the surrounding nebula, producing the enormous envelopes of gas that characterise the giant planets.

3. Gas Giants and Ice Giants

The four giants fall into two groups. Jupiter and Saturn, the gas giants, are composed mainly of hydrogen and helium in proportions close to those of the Sun. Together they hold more than 90 percent of all the mass in the Solar System outside the Sun itself. Uranus and Neptune, the ice giants, are much smaller, with a combined mass of about 31 Earth masses, and contain a larger share of heavier compounds such as water, ammonia and methane.

4. Jupiter

Jupiter is the largest planet, with a radius of about 71,000 kilometres, and orbits at an average distance of 5.2 AU. Its mass is greater than that of all the other planets combined. It has well over a hundred confirmed moons, including the four large Galilean satellites discovered in the early seventeenth century. One of them, Ganymede, is the largest moon in the Solar System and is larger in diameter than the planet Mercury, although it is less massive.

5. Saturn

Saturn orbits between about 9.1 and 10.1 AU from the Sun. It possesses the most prominent ring system of any planet, the only one easily visible from the Earth through a small telescope. Saturn also has the largest number of confirmed moons. Its largest moon, Titan, is likewise larger than Mercury and is notable for its thick atmosphere.

6. Uranus

Uranus orbits between about 18.3 and 20.1 AU. Its most unusual feature is its axial tilt, which exceeds 90 degrees, so that the planet effectively rotates on its side relative to its orbit. This tilt produces extreme seasons in which each pole faces the Sun for decades at a time. Uranus has a faint ring system and more than two dozen known moons.

7. Neptune

Neptune is the outermost planet, orbiting at about 30 AU. It was identified in 1846 after irregularities in the orbit of Uranus pointed to the gravitational pull of an unseen planet. Its largest moon, Triton, orbits in the direction opposite to Neptune's rotation. Such a retrograde orbit suggests that Triton did not form around Neptune but was captured, possibly from the population of icy bodies beyond the planets.

8. Rings and Migration

All four giant planets have ring systems, ranging from Saturn's broad, bright rings to the narrow, dark rings of Uranus and Neptune. Their orbits, meanwhile, may not be where they began. The Nice model proposes that interactions between the giant planets and a disc of leftover planetesimals caused them to migrate and passed through a phase of instability before settling into their current orbits. The grand tack hypothesis adds that Jupiter migrated inward and then back outward, reshaping the region that became the asteroid belt.

9. Conclusion

The giant planets owe their size to the abundance of ice beyond the frost line, which allowed rapid growth and the capture of nebular gas. Their rings, their many moons and their likely history of migration make them central to understanding how the Solar System as a whole reached its present arrangement.
